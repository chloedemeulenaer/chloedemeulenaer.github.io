When people disagree on economic policy, is it because they differ in their values, or in what they think those policies do? Using a large survey of almost 8,000 French respondents, I measure support for 29 economic reforms along with beliefs about their incidence, their impact on the budget and on the macroeconomy (“policy impact beliefs”). Participants favour reforms that they believe benefit them, and benefit minimum wage workers; however the strongest predictor of support is the reform’s perceived impact on economic growth, which dominates even self-interest. Policy impact beliefs explain more variation in support than a range of variables shown to robustly predict support for redistribution, such as ideology or zero-sum thinking. Half of the explained partisan gap is due to differences in policy impact beliefs—left- and right-wing participants have a similar mapping of beliefs to support, except for the greater weight put by left-wing individuals on the wellbeing of welfare recipients. Differences in policy impact beliefs also explain most of the explained preference for predistributive reforms over redistributive ones. Compared to the economic literature, participants underestimate the positive effects of expansionary reforms; providing research-based information does not significantly correct misperceptions, but does increase support for progressive reforms. This project provides a unified framework to analyse support for economic policies; one that sheds light on both between-group and between-policy differences in support.
