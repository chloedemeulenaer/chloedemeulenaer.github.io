When people disagree on economic policy, is it because they differ in their values, or in what they think those policies do? I measure support for 29 economic reforms in a large survey, along with beliefs about their incidence, impact on the budget and impact on the macroeconomy. Participants favour reforms that they believe benefit them and minimum wage workers; however the strongest predictor of support is the reform’s perceived impact on economic growth. These policy impact beliefs explain more variation in support than a range of variables shown to robustly predict demand for redistribution, such as ideology or zero-sum thinking. Policy impact beliefs shed light on both between-group and between-policy differences in support: half of the explained partisan gap is due to differences in policy impact beliefs—left- and right-wing participants have a similar mapping of beliefs to support, except for the greater weight put by left-wing individuals on the wellbeing of welfare recipients. Differences in policy impact beliefs also account for most of the explained preference for predistributive reforms over redistributive ones. I find evidence of biases in those beliefs: compared to the economic literature, participants systematically underestimate the positive effects of expansionary reforms.
